Nuclear & Firm Clean Power for AI Data Centers

Triggered by: CEG — mapped to Nuclear & Firm Clean Power for AI Data Centers · Buy-side thematic read · October 8, 2026 · Theme Conviction 6/10 · Peak verdict: Intact

1. Introduction

  • What it is: firm, around-the-clock power sold on long contracts to artificial intelligence (AI) data centers — first the existing US nuclear fleet (contracts, restarts, uprates), then new reactors and the fuel cycle behind them, with gas-fired plants as the bridge that fills the gap.
  • Why now: US data center power demand is forecast to climb from 31 gigawatts (GW) in 2025 to 41 GW in 2026 and 66 GW in 2027, while the largest US grid (PJM) cleared its 2028/29 capacity auction at the $325 per megawatt-day cap and still finished 6,831 megawatts (MW) short of its reliability requirement; heavy-duty gas turbines are now booked into 2031 (GE Vernova reservation backlog 116 GW).
  • End state: by the early 2030s, hyperscalers hold 20-year contracts on roughly 9.8 GW of nuclear output (13 announced projects as of May 2026), Three Mile Island Unit 1 (Crane) is back online for Microsoft in 2027, the first commercial advanced reactor (TerraPower's Natrium) is running, and US nuclear capacity is on a path from ~100 GW toward the 400 GW national goal for 2050 (Executive Order 14302).
  • Investment opportunity: Meta alone signed up to 6.6 GW of nuclear supply by 2035 (Vistra, TerraPower, Oklo; Jan 9, 2026), the Department of Energy (DOE) issued a conditional loan commitment of up to $17.5B for 10 large reactors (Jun 23, 2026), and a government–Brookfield–Cameco partnership targets $80B of Westinghouse reactors; unlike prior hype cycles the buyers are investment-grade hyperscalers on take-or-pay contracts, not merchant spot exposure.
  • The market has already started to separate the theme into two trades: contracted generators with real earnings (Constellation, Vistra, Talen) versus pre-revenue reactor developers whose first new-build power arrives in 2029–2032 at the earliest — Constellation is −21.8% year to date and Oklo −55.6% against +14.2% for the S&P 500 (SPY).
  • Common misconception: nuclear is not a pure AI-capex derivative; regulation decides how much of the premium the generators keep — the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) denied Talen's expanded supply to Amazon on grid-reliability and consumer-cost grounds and, on Sep 29, 2026, suspended PJM's backstop capacity auction through Feb 28, 2027.

Technology Inflection — ⚠️ Partial

  • Proven today: Crane (Three Mile Island Unit 1) is on track to restart in 2027 after FERC approved the transfer of interconnection rights and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) approved the fuel license amendment; DOE is lending up to $1B (Constellation, 2026).
  • Uprates add output at existing plants: the Meta–Clinton 20-year PPA, starting June 2027, funds a 30 MW uprate (Constellation Q2 2026).
  • New build is not a cost-curve break yet: only NuScale's 77 MWe design has full NRC design certification (Jan 2023); TerraPower's Natrium has the first commercial advanced-reactor construction permit and targets the early 2030s; first-wave SMR operation realistically reaches 2029–2032 (SMR Intel).
  • Fuel is the real technology gap: Centrus produced only ~900 kg of high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU) in 2025, a fraction of one SMR core; it signed a $900M fixed-price DOE contract on Jun 30, 2026 to build commercial-scale capacity at Piketon.

Regulatory Shift — ✅ Firing

  • Executive Order 14302 (May 23, 2025) sets a goal of ~300 GW of new capacity, reaching 400 GW by 2050 from ~100 GW today; DOE announced a conditional loan commitment of up to $17.5B for 10 large reactors (Jun 23, 2026) and an $80B Westinghouse partnership with Brookfield and Cameco.
  • The NRC is compressing timelines: it agreed to an 18-month review target for X-energy's Xe-100 construction permit (possible by late 2026), and TerraPower's permit took about two years.
  • The door also closes: FERC rejected Talen's expanded power allocation to Amazon, warning of "huge ramifications for both grid reliability and consumer costs" — a ruling that, by one account, cost Constellation more than 10% of its market cap.
  • On Sep 29, 2026 FERC suspended PJM's Reliability Backstop Procurement through Feb 28, 2027, citing flaws in cost allocation and collateral rules, leaving no timetable for securing up to 6.8 GW (FERC order ER26-3380).

Behavioral Change — ✅ Firing

  • Thirteen announced projects commit more than 9.8 GW of nuclear capacity to AI infrastructure as of May 2026 (SMR Intel); Meta (6.6 GW by 2035) now outranks Amazon, Microsoft and Google as the largest buyer.
  • Microsoft has a 20-year PPA for the full output of the 835 MW Crane restart; Google has a 25-year, 615 MW PPA to bring Duane Arnold (Iowa) back; Amazon holds up to 1.92 GW from Talen's Susquehanna plant through 2042.
  • Behavior is not reverting: buyers are accepting 20-plus-year take-or-pay terms for power they could previously source on demand, because interconnection queues and turbine lead times (3+ years at Siemens Energy; GE Vernova slots for 2031) make speed the scarce input.
  • Counterparties are the strongest in the market — Microsoft, Meta, Amazon and Alphabet plan roughly $725B of combined 2026 capex (+77% year over year) and Wall Street sees 2027 above $1 trillion.

Capital Flow — ✅ Firing

  • Nuclear-sector investment reached $2.1B in Q2 2026 and the Global X Uranium ETF (URA) recorded its largest quarterly net inflow since 2021, $623M (Trackinsight, SMR Intel).
  • DOE awarded $2.7B of enrichment orders over 10 years — $900M each to Centrus's American Centrifuge Operating, Orano Federal Services and General Matter — and Centrus ended Q2 2026 with a $4.5B backlog through 2040.
  • Constellation completed the Calpine acquisition on Jan 7, 2026, creating a 55 GW fleet (nuclear, natural gas and geothermal) and the largest US power producer; strategic M&A (buying rather than building) is late-cycle confidence.
  • Pre-revenue developers are funding themselves in public markets: Oklo filed a $1.5B at-the-market equity program and NuScale relies on repeated at-the-market sales (Orrick; Nasdaq) — capital is concentrated, but increasingly via dilution.

Narrative Momentum — ✅ Firing

  • Nuclear is now a standing item on hyperscaler agendas and in US industrial policy; the Trackinsight and AIC coverage describes a nuclear "sea change" in utility funds.
  • The narrative has peaked for the pre-revenue names: Oklo (−55.6% year to date, −80% from its 52-week high), NuScale (−55.1%), Nano Nuclear (−44.7%) and Centrus (−47.9%) while Constellation is −21.8% (Yahoo Finance, Oct 8, 2026).
  • Press coverage has turned to the "which of these has a real business" question — Oklo fell 28% in a month into mid-July 2026 (247WallSt, Motley Fool), while Constellation and Vistra held up better.
  • Nuclear exchange-traded funds still draw capital (inflows of $4.9M in early Q4 despite the downturn), so attention has not left; it has moved from the story names to the earners.

4. TAM Expansion Analysis

4a. Market Size & Growth Drivers

AI data centers create net-new firm-power demand rather than redistributing existing spend: the load is large enough that the largest US grid is already short of its reliability target and the buyers will contract 20 years ahead for supply. The legacy nuclear business was a flat 100 GW fleet selling into volatile power markets; the new market is contracted, premium-priced capacity plus a new-build and fuel cycle.

  • US data center power demand: 31 GW (2025) → 41 GW (2026) → 66 GW (2027); 13.6 GW of capacity scheduled for 2026 and 36.3 GW for 2027 (Goldman Sachs via industry coverage, 2026).
  • Morgan Stanley: US data center demand could reach 74 GW by 2028, with a 45–49 GW shortfall in available grid access.
  • PJM 2028/29 capacity auction: cleared at the $325/MW-day cap, 6,831 MW short of the reliability requirement; the 2026/27 auction cleared at $329.17/MW-day, with about $6.3B of the $16.4B auction value attributed to data-center demand (PJM; Power Magazine).
  • Nuclear capacity goal: ~100 GW today → 400 GW by 2050 (Executive Order 14302); DOE $17.5B conditional loan for 10 reactors (Jun 23, 2026).
  • Uranium: demand 65,000 tonnes U3O8 (2025) → 85,000 tonnes (2030) against ~58,000 tonnes of primary supply; spot $92.50/lb and term $98.00/lb in June 2026 (Skillings, 2026).
  • Hyperscaler capex: ~$725B in 2026 (+77% on $410B in 2025), with 2027 forecasts above $1 trillion (Evercore, Bank of America; Moody's).

Structural drivers are the physical ones — grid interconnection queues, three-year-plus turbine and 128-week transformer lead times, and the licensing time for new reactors; none of them clears with cheap money. Cyclical drivers are the pace of AI capex, the policy price collar in PJM ($325/MW-day for two more auctions) and the cost of capital, which has moved against long-duration assets: the 10-year Treasury yield hit 5.04% on Sep 15, 2026.
